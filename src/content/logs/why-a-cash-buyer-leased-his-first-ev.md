## The old method

Our car method was simple, and it worked for a long time: buy a low-to-medium priced car with cash, pick something reliable with good resale, and hold it. Hold it past the steep part of the depreciation curve, because the longer you keep a car, the cheaper each month gets. That's the long tail, and it's the whole trick.

The Tiguan was the best version of that trick. We bought it during Dieselgate, at a huge discount, with an extended seven-year warranty. The warranty is what made holding it long feel safe, not just cheap. I wanted to keep it even longer, because every extra year would have pushed the monthly cost down further.

Then it got totaled, and the long tail got cut off.

|  |  |
|---|---|
| Bought, Dieselgate discount | $23,000 |
| Insurance payout, gross | $13,800 |
| Less deductible | $1,000 |
| Net back | $12,800 |
| Loss over about 8 years | $10,200 |
| Monthly depreciation | ~$106/mo |

So about $100 a month, for eight years of family car. That's the yardstick I was measuring everything else against. And suddenly we needed a replacement, and the replacement was an EV, and every rule that used to make the decision easy stopped working.

## Why that method breaks on an EV

My wife test-drove the Kia EV3 Wind and liked it, more or less. That "more or less" mattered — it was the only one that fit. The EV6 was too sporty, the EV9 too big, and we were done with VW after the Tiguan, so no ID.4. A used Audi Q4 was on the list and came off it.

But an EV breaks the old method in three ways at once. First, battery technology is moving fast. Buying a car and holding it for ten years feels like locking in today's battery. Second, the incentives are moving fast too. California's MyFirstEV rebate had only launched in August — $3,500 at point of sale, $1,750 from the state and $1,750 matched by Kia, separate from Kia's own factory lease cash — and Kia's program numbers changed from September to October, with the advertised lease expiring in early November. You couldn't just do last month's math and trust it.

Third, gas itself was part of the push. For future-me reading this in ten years: in late October 2026, regular gas in California was about $6.03 a gallon, premium about $6.46, and San Francisco regular was $6.26. The WRX drinks premium, so every commute leg in that car was getting expensive in a way that was hard to ignore. That didn't make the EV a money-saver on its own — at my wife's mileage, it wasn't — but it made looking at one unavoidable.

And then, the part nobody warns you about: you don't just buy the car. You have to figure out how to feed it. More on that below.

## The dealer sheet

We started with Dublin Kia. Their car was in stock, Interstellar Gray, the one she wanted. Their lease sheet said $382 a month plus tax, $2,000 due at signing, $17,250 total over three years. It looked plausible until you took it apart line by line.

The money factor was marked up. The factory lease cash was short. I checked it against Kia's own calculator, and against the October program numbers other buyers were reporting, and the sheet didn't survive that check. I first thought the gap was even bigger than it was, because one of the lease-cash figures I found was quoted for Pennsylvania, not California. The clean sheet from Hayward later proved the real California number. That's worth saying plainly: the Dublin sheet was still padded, but not by as much as my first, angriest version of the math.

|  | Dublin Kia | Hayward Kia, signed |
|---|---|---|
| Term | 36 months / 12k miles a year | 36 months / 10k miles a year |
| Money factor | 0.00269 | 0.00229, the October buy rate |
| Rebates | $1,500 Kia cash + $3,500 MyFirstEV | $2,400 factory lease cash + $3,500 MyFirstEV |
| Monthly, incl. tax | $423.61 | $346.87 |
| Due at signing | $2,000 | $2,375 |
| Total, same accounting | $17,249.96 | ~$14,500–14,900 |

Dublin Kia was the first EV dealer we'd actually gone to check, so we walked out rather than haggling it in the showroom. The follow-up — the lease sheet, the back-and-forth on it — happened by text message. Then I found Leasehackr.com on Reddit, a pre-negotiated Bay Area deal, and the dealer behind it turned out to be Hayward Kia. Their internet sales manager, Kassidy, sent a worksheet, and this time every number checked out: buy-rate money factor, both rebates applied, the $1,198 in accessories exactly as disclosed, no junk add-ons, standard California fees only. We signed on October 21, drove it home that afternoon, and parked it next to the WRX.

We took the 10k version, not the 12k version, even though I'd first leaned toward 12k. The split plan justifies it: the EV3 takes one of my 23-mile commute legs a week, charged at work, the WRX takes the other, and my wife drives about 12 miles a day locally. That's roughly 7,300 miles a year projected, comfortably under the cap. The total came in around $14,000 over three years, under the $15,000–17,000 target I'd set.

## How it drives

We drove it, and it's nice. Quiet, smooth acceleration, good power consumption, good enough for a family car. That's the honest version: as an appliance, it does its job well, and it does it without drama.

It's just less engaging than my WRX. No surprise there, and no complaint either — that's not what this car is for. The EV3 is the car that gets used every day so the WRX doesn't have to be. Less engaging is, in this fleet, a feature.

## The three-year bet

Leasing, for us, is not a new philosophy. It's a three-year option. I hope the tariff wall looks different by 2029, when the lease ends, because Chinese EVs are nicer and cheaper than what's available here, and the real American gap isn't car factories — we have plenty of those — it's battery manufacturing capacity. It doesn't matter much which way the cost comes down, lower tariffs or more competition between American-made and Chinese-owned American-made EVs, as long as it does. And if the EV3 is still a good car in three years, with low miles on it, a buyout is one way back to the old hold-pattern. If it isn't, we hand it back and the fast-moving technology problem was never ours to own.

## The charging puzzle

Then came the charging puzzle, which turned out to be the most interesting problem in the whole purchase, and the one I most want future-me to remember correctly.

I started out thinking we didn't need an install at all. Twelve miles a day, a regular 120V outlet overnight, that should have been plenty. Then the portable charger plugged into the garage outlet showed FAULT and wouldn't charge at all. The house is old, the garage panel is original, and a lot of the branch wiring out there simply isn't grounded the way a modern EV charger expects.

That sent me hunting for a cheaper way through, and I found two candidates. Future-me, if you're reading this in ten years and wondering why I was haggling over a few hundred dollars on a $36,000 car: I wasn't always like this. I came back from Sweden and learned all that from my cousin. That's where this habit came from.

|  | Cheap workaround | Doing it right |
|---|---|---|
| What | Found a ground wire in the old 3-prong dryer box, convert it to a 4-prong NEMA 14-30 with grounding, add a NeoCharge splitter | Permitted, hardwired wall-mount fast charger |
| Charge | 240V, 24A | Fast charger, hardwired |
| Cost, my estimate | ~$350 after rebate | Easily over $1,200+ — the permit alone was quoted at $580, plus a $120 assessment |
| Catch | The outlet fix is clean; the splitter is the contested part, and the rebate question is still open | The price is the blocker, and the payback runs past the 36-month lease |

The "doing it right" version isn't about an oven breaker — that breaker is just panel detail, a space that looked unused. The real contrast is cheap versus right, and right is expensive because the permit is expensive. For now, the car runs on the interim plan: work charging, which is invite-only at $0.25/kWh, plus public EVgo charging for my wife's local miles. That's roughly $816–869 a year for energy, versus about $505 a year if home charging ever happens on off-peak rates. Not nothing, but not a reason to rush a $1,200 install that pays back after the car is gone.

## Where the fleet stands

So this is where the fleet stands. The EV3 does appliance duty. The WRX, the garage queen, carries the old mission now: hold it long, let Subaru's resale reputation do its work, give it the highway stretch it needs, and keep it special. The Tiguan taught us the method. The EV3 taught us what happens when the method meets a technology that won't sit still, incentives that won't sit still, gas at $6 a gallon, and a house that won't charge a car.

In 2029, we'll see which bet was right.
